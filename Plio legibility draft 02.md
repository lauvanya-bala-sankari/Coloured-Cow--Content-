# Nothing Is Broken. So Why Does Everything Feel Slower?

You built something real. It started as an idea, and now it's live, growing the way products do — one feature at a time, each one making sense on its own. A dashboard gets added, then reporting, then permissions, then a second kind of user. Every decision was the right call when it was made. The product works. People depend on it.

What gets harder to hold onto is the picture of how it all fits together — a picture that started small, in a handful of people's heads, and grew through conversation rather than documentation, at its own pace. Eventually, everyone can explain their own part. What gets harder is explaining the whole. 

We watched a version of this play out in Plio, an education platform(https://www.plio.in/) we partnered with from 2021 to 2023. Plio already had a working product when they came to us — it had run for months, with real users, the idea already proven. They knew exactly where they wanted to take the platform next.They'd also already concluded, on their own, that the foundation needed rebuilding — and that it wasn't expertise they had in-house. They weren't looking for a build service, either. They wanted to know what was actually being built, and to sit inside the technical decisions that would shape where the platform went. Along the way they'd run into a list of problems — components breaking, integrations giving way, shipping getting slower — but underneath all of them was a harder question: could the system, as it stood, actually carry the product where they wanted it to go?

That need shaped the first month. The team produced fewer features and more of a description: a definition of the product, the first designs, and the technical specifications underneath them, including the database schema. To build that new schema, the existing product had to be understood first — so the team went through it field by field, workflow by workflow, asking one question of each part: what is this for? Two answers kept coming back. This isn't needed at all. Or, this needs a simpler form. Nobody set out to simplify the product. But once every part had to be explained out loud, some of them turned out not to have a good reason for being there. What came out the other side was smaller, and still did the same job — and Plio's own team was in the room for it, not waiting on the other side of a handoff.

The same need for a clear picture showed up again in testing, but with a much wider set of conditions to account for. Plio was always going to be open source — not to attract outside contributors, but so other organizations could adopt the platform and run it themselves. Plio's teachers worked mostly in controlled desktop settings. Students were the ones reaching the platform from everywhere — a wide range of phones, often a parent's, across Android browsers that don't all behave quite the same. That range, together with every future organization that would meet it without this team anywhere nearby to notice if something broke, was what the tests actually had to survive. Documentation could tell another organization how the platform was supposed to work. Only the tests could show them that it did. So rather than test for everything and imply a certainty nobody had, the team said plainly what had been covered and what hadn't — because in rooms none of them would ever stand in, including Plio's own, the tests were how the system explained itself.

Across all of this, the answer was never a single fix. It was the same habit, applied again and again: making the system explainable before making it bigger.

So the reasoning behind the new schema couldn't just be correct. It had to be something Plio's own team could follow and hold onto. When the work wrapped up in 2023, that's what carried forward — the platform kept moving under Plio's own team, running a system they'd understood the whole time, not one they'd simply inherited.

If any of this sounds like where your product is right now — still working, just harder to explain than it used to be — you're not behind. What's worth asking from here is simple: can the people running it still explain, honestly, how it holds together?
