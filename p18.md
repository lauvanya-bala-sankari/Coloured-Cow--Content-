Publishing the Code Was the Easy Part

You built something real. It started as an idea, and now it's live — people use it, it does what you set out to make it do, and you can point to the exact shape it's taken because of decisions you made along the way. Getting here took real technical judgment and real design calls, made under real constraints, and you made enough of them well to actually arrive.

Now there's a newer question sitting on your desk, and it doesn't feel like the ones you've already answered. Should this be open source? The case sounds clean when it's said out loud — transparency, community, less vendor lock-in, maybe some goodwill along the way. It's tempting to treat it as mostly a licensing choice. Flip the repository to public, write a README, done.

We watched that assumption get tested properly in Plio, an education platform we partnered with from 2021 to 2023. Plio was always going to be open source — not as a branding choice, but because it needed to reduce long-term risk and let other organizations actually adopt and run the platform themselves.

The question didn't arrive as a technical decision. It surfaced quietly, underneath other conversations — about priorities shifting, funding changing, the current team not always being around in the same way. Underneath all of it was one real question: how does this software survive beyond the specific people currently responsible for it?

The instinct, early on, was the obvious one: open code means transparency, transparency means trust, trust means safety. It's a clean chain of reasoning. It didn't hold up under any real weight. Publishing code didn't actually answer who reviews contributions, who fixes issues once interest drops, or who's accountable when something breaks in production for someone who adopted the platform months later. Visibility on its own didn't create any of that. It just meant more people could see a problem without anyone being responsible for solving it.

That reframed what open source actually was. It doesn't reduce the amount of responsibility a team carries — it redistributes it, and often increases it. A codebase that's technically open but practically unsupported is worse than one that's honestly closed, because it offers a false sense of safety to whoever adopts it, and only disappoints them later, once they're already depending on it.

So the work slowed down before the repository ever went public. Documentation quality, modularity, security posture, governance — all of it got treated as prerequisites, not afterthoughts. That wasn't hesitation. Delaying openness was the more responsible decision, because rushing it would have handed something unfinished to organizations that had no way of knowing it was unfinished. The answer wasn't no. It was not yet, and not without the groundwork.

What actually shipped alongside the code was the thing that made it usable by someone the team would never meet — documentation structured so another organization could genuinely understand and adopt the system, not just read it. That structural work is what reduced the long-term risk in the first place, and it's also what let Plio be handed to other organizations without duplicating infrastructure or tightly coupling their operations to any one team.

None of that reads as a statement of values. It reads as a promise kept over time. For a platform built for social impact, the cost of being abandoned by its own maintainers is higher than the cost of staying dependent on them — and open source only actually helps if it reduces that risk, rather than just looking like it has.

Publishing the code was the easy half.
